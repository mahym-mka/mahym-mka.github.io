Before Recycling

Recovery is the prerequisite to recycling.
Originally written for coursework and subsequently revised for this portfolio. © Mahym Alekberova.

Scroll through climate posts, videos, studies, or social media feeds and you'll likely encounter another creative solution to plastic waste. Plastics can be turned into fuel, consumed by engineered bacteria, recovered from oceans, incorporated into pavement, or transformed into jewelry, construction materials, and countless other products. These innovations are often presented as ways to curb plastics' growing presence in landfills and marine environments.

Public discussions of plastic waste generally revolve around two responses. The first is reduction: use fewer bags, bottles, forks, straws, and other single-use products. The second is recycling: collect discarded plastics and return them to productive use. Both approaches have merit. Yet both can obscure a more basic reality. Plastic cannot be repurposed, recycled, or reinvented if it is first lost from systems capable of recovering it.

At first glance, reducing plastic use appears straightforward. Yet plastics remain indispensable to modern life. They support medical procedures, food preservation, construction, communications infrastructure, and countless everyday products. Their durability presents a paradox as well. A material capable of serving society for decades in water systems, buildings, and communications networks can also become an environmental burden when used for products intended to last only minutes. The same properties that make plastics valuable help explain why discarded plastics continue to accumulate long after their usefulness has ended.

These characteristics help explain why reducing and recycling plastics are more complicated than they first appear.

Recycling presents a different challenge. The existence of recycling systems is not the same as the effectiveness of recycling systems. Plastic must first be collected, separated, retained, and kept accessible before it can enter recycling streams in the first place. In that chain, plastics are often contaminated, and many short-use plastics (plastic bags, disposable utensils, and similar items) are excluded from recycling streams to begin with. Long before plastic can be transformed into any alternative use via recycling or repurposing, it must first remain available to those processes.

Before a material can be recycled, it must first remain recoverable.

The challenge is to prevent plastics from becoming dispersed throughout ecosystems, buried in unmanaged waste, or fragmented into microplastics before their value can be recovered.

This is where recent developments in Artificial Intelligence (AI) offer a useful reframe. AI does not create value from information that does not exist. Its power comes from finding new patterns, connections, and uses within information that has already been collected, organized, and retained. Its power is combinatorial.

Recycling can be understood in much the same way. It creates value by finding new uses for plastic that already exists. A plastic bottle may become pavement, construction materials, fuel, or an input to technologies yet to be invented. Like AI, however, that transformation depends on retaining access to the underlying resource. Human ingenuity cannot repurpose what it cannot recover.

A deleted archive cannot be analyzed. A buried bottle cannot be reused.

If recovery is the prerequisite to recycling, then the question becomes what systems best enable recovery.

At the local level, this could begin with rethinking what we currently call recycling bins. Their purpose is often framed as sorting waste, yet durable plastics that retain value after use are better understood as recoverable materials. Recovery bins could prioritize capture over precision. Instead of asking households to wash, sort, and judge the recyclability of every item, plastics could be collected and sent to centralized cleaning and processing facilities. What is cumbersome for one household can become efficient when done in bulk, reducing demands on water, time, and effort while preserving more material for future use.

Between households and recycling facilities, intermediary recovery hubs could serve a similar function. Located near retail centers, transfer stations, warehouses, or community collection points, they could aggregate, sort, and store materials before they enter downstream recycling or repurposing streams. Their role could be indispensable to preventing potentially valuable materials from becoming unrecoverable.

At a regional level, larger facilities could process recovered plastics into feedstocks for manufacturing, construction materials, chemical recycling, or uses not yet imagined. Just as wholesalers connect producers and retailers, recovery systems could connect discarded materials with future users. Between disposal and reuse lies an entire chain of economic activity: collection, transportation, sorting, cleaning, storage, processing, and repurposing. Recovery aligns environmental management with economic impact.

Whether such systems are viable at scale remains an empirical question. Municipalities, businesses, researchers, and development institutions could test recovery-centered approaches through pilot programs and randomized trials. Do recovery-focused collection systems retain more material than traditional recycling programs? Does centralized cleaning outperform (and save more water than) household cleaning? Do intermediary recovery hubs increase reuse and recycling rates? These are questions that can be answered through experimentation rather than assumption.

Human ingenuity has already demonstrated countless ways discarded plastics can be transformed into something useful. The challenge now may be less about inventing the next solution and more about ensuring that sufficient material remains available for those solutions to act upon. Before plastic can become fuel, pavement, jewelry, construction material, or the input to technologies yet to be invented, it must first remain available to become something else.

That is, before recycling, there is recovery.
